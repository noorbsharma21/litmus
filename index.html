Litmus

A taste layer for AI.

Creating a slop buster.

Everything I know but never wrote down, so a model can match my taste.

What is taste

Robert Zajonc ran an experiment in 1968. He showed people shapes they had never seen before, some once, some a dozen times. They liked the ones they had seen most. Later work found it holds even when people cannot remember seeing the image at all.

Bourdieu applied it to people rather than shapes. Nobody picks their taste. You absorb it from whatever surrounded you, then spend your life calling it a preference.

You like what you have already been looking at. My camera roll is a record of what has been around me.

Here is mine.

Four things keep coming back. Written as instructions, because that is the form they have to end up in.

Set things inside a field, not on a background

Choose the ground first and give it a treatment of its own: a colour, a texture, another image. The border is part of the work, not the space left over.

If colour is in, it takes the whole area

Flood the field. Never use colour to pick one element out against a neutral. Either the ground is coloured and everything sits inside it, or the page is black on white.

Nothing is twice the size of anything else

No centred subject, no hero image, no single focal point. Come at it from the side: the back rather than the face, the shadow rather than the person.

Drawn and photographed get the same frame

Same size, same crop, same weight. A screenshot sits beside an illustration with no hierarchy between them.

The problem

Last semester I built a lot of interfaces with Claude. Each one started with forty minutes of corrections before it made anything I would keep: no purple gradient, no centred heading, no rounded corners. By the fifth project I was pasting the same paragraph into an empty chat.

The building was never the problem. Ask for six versions and you get six, all usable, and it has no view on which one is worth keeping. That comes back to you every time.

If you do not supply the view, you get the middle of everything. People call that slop.

The idea

Taste works like a limit. What makes work yours is the long list of things you will not do. That list never gets written anywhere, so it never reaches the prompt.

Litmus writes it down. You say what you are making. Everything else is already decided.

Four things it holds

The refusals

What I will not do, said outright. A model will do anything you have not forbidden, so this part comes first.

The rules

Type, colour, alignment, weight. The decisions I would otherwise retype at the start of every session.

The silhouette

The shape the work sits in before anything is drawn. Spacing, proportion, measure, how much air a page gets. Not tied to one kind of design, which is why it carries across a poster, a site and an email.

The corrections

Every time I reject something it goes back in. The file gets closer to me the more I work, without me writing anything new.

How I am building it

I am not writing my taste down from memory. I make things and record what I decide while I make them.

Five or six pieces to start. Two websites, an image, a poster, an email. Every choice gets logged, including the ones I threw out. The thrown out ones teach it more.

Twelve of the fifty-three pieces in my art portfolio. All drawn by hand, years before any of this. The cleanest record of what I do when nothing is instructing me.

  1. Design 5 or 6 things myself. Two websites, an image, a poster, an email. Real pieces, finished properly, not exercises.
  2. Log every decision, kept and thrown out. The four headlines that died. The colour I tried for ten minutes and hated. A finished piece only shows what I kept. The rejects show where the limit is.
  3. Write the never list and the always list. Said outright. Never a purple gradient. Never a centred headline. Always left aligned. Always one loud thing at most. The never list does more work, because a model will do anything you have not forbidden.
  4. Feed it back and mark what it got wrong. It reads the decisions and the lists and tells me what it thinks my taste is. I correct it. The corrections matter most.
  5. Write the file. One page. Never list first, then the rules, then exact sizes and colours, then a short test it can run on its own work.
  6. Test it on something new. A brief it has not seen, built twice, with the file and without. If I cannot tell which is which, it has not learned anything. Back to step three.

Saved references sit underneath as background, but they are weaker. What I made says more than what I admire.

What the research says

I am not the first to try this. Four findings changed how I am building it.

What the papers found

Asking why works

ViPer has people comment on a handful of images saying what they like and dislike, then a model turns that into a structured list of liked and disliked attributes. Close to what I am doing, and it holds up.

A few examples are enough

Personalized Preference Fine-tuning of Diffusion Models personalises from a few ranked pairs and wins 76% of the time against the baseline. I do not need thousands of judgments.

The gap is predictable

PAMELA collected 70,000 ratings on 5,000 images, fifteen people each, and found how far one person sits from generic aesthetics can be modelled. That is exactly step 02: throw away what every designer would say and keep the gap.

Dislikes set the boundary

Work on negative preference in recommenders shows what someone rejects constrains the answer in a way likes cannot. This is why the never list comes first in the file.

The gap in all of it: these systems learn a preference and keep it inside the model. None of them hand you a file you can read, argue with and carry to another tool.

What the first read got wrong

The first attempt said my backgrounds are always off-white and my type should be distinctive. Both were wrong, and I only found out by seeing them built. I said so and they were rewritten.

The method is supposed to do that. The first read of anyone's taste describes good design in general. It becomes theirs once they say what it got wrong.

What you get

Two things, and you choose which you want.

A prompt. Copy it, paste it, use it once. Good for a single piece.

A skill. One page of instructions you drop into a project folder. Every chat about that project then starts with your taste already loaded, and you never type the setup again. This is the one I use.

The skill is printed below. It is short because a model skims anything longer.

My taste, version two. Noor Sharma.

NEVER  no exceptions
  No rounded corners past 2px.
  No centred headline. Everything left.
  No gradients, no glow, no frosted glass, no blur.
  No drop shadows.
  No texture laid over the top. Real material or nothing.
  Nothing moves when the page scrolls.
  Never write: supercharge, effortlessly, introducing, seamless.

TYPE
  One typeface, used at many sizes. Never three typefaces.
  Body text 16px. Do not make it bigger.
  One loud element per page, at most.

COLOUR
  Background plain. Black text, full strength, not grey.
  One accent colour, used three times at most.
  Colour comes from the photographs, or not at all.

COMPOSITION
  Set things inside a field, never on a background.
  Give the ground its own treatment. The border is part of the work.
  If colour is in, it takes the whole area. No accents against neutral.
  Nothing is twice the size of anything else. No hero image.
  Drawn and photographed get the same frame, same weight.

LAYOUT
  Left aligned. One column. Numbered sections.
  Lines and rules hold the page together. Nothing floats.
  Pack it tight. Put real words on the page.

SILHOUETTE
  One measure, 640px. Do not widen it for long text.
  30px between sections. 14px between paragraphs.
  Air goes around a block, never inside it.
  Same proportions whether it is a page, a poster or an email.

What changed

  • The purple heading, the little pill badge and the three rounded cards are gone. All three are on the never list.
  • Centred everything became a composed page with one image carrying it.
  • The words stopped selling. Places for the particular is a point of view, not a benefit.
  • Numbered steps replaced feature boxes.

Open questions

  1. What should the final output actually be? A prompt is a thin version of it. A skill is a bit better. A design system file is another answer. I want to hand over something you can use again and again without starting from scratch, and I do not know yet which form that is.
  2. Should the rules change with the subject? A wine shop and a student club are different jobs. Keeping the rules fixed is the stronger claim. Letting them move is more useful, and then somebody has to decide how they move.

How you can help

Four things I want to know

How do you prompt for design?

Show me what you actually type when you want an LLM to make something look a certain way. Paste it as it is, including the parts you repeat every time.

What output would help you most?

A prompt, a file, a finished page, a set of options. If this tool handed you one thing, what should it be.

Which stage do you struggle with?

Starting, choosing between versions, or finishing. I want to know where the work actually stalls for you.

How much would you change it?

When it gives you something back, how editable does it need to be. A locked answer, a starting point, or every value exposed.
