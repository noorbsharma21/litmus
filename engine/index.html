Stage one. Reading my own work to find out what my taste is, then writing it down where a model can use it.

What goes in

Not references I admire. Work I made, which is harder to fake and says more. Drawings and paintings from the portfolio, photographs off my phone, and pages I designed and built.

Every piece has been read and tagged. Click any one to open its record.

What the engine takes off each piece

Every piece gets read for the same things: what the ground is, how colour behaves, how the frame is used, what is repeated, what the subject is. Tags in black are particular. Tags in grey are things any competent designer would also do, and they get thrown away in the next step.

The things that are not pictures

A corpus of images only teaches the engine how things look. Most of what I actually go back to is a song or a poem, and what carries over from those is structure: how long before it gets going, how much is left out, whether it repeats, how it ends. Those are layout questions in a different costume, so they are asked that way.

These are not my work, so they sit apart from the corpus and are labelled that way in the file. They bias what the engine reaches for. They do not become rules.

What I turn down on sight

The corpus says what I have made. This says what I refuse, which is the stronger signal: a no draws a boundary round the answer, a yes only marks a point inside it. Ninety four specimens across seven categories, in two rounds.

Each one is shown three ways, because a decision is rarely right or wrong on its own. Heavy type is right for a headline and wrong for body copy. Answer all three at once when the verdict is the same everywhere, or click the tiles one by one when it is not. A split answer writes a conditional rule instead of a flat one. The reason box is worth using: the comment turns out to be more useful than the verdict.

Click a tile to flip it between yes and no. Or answer all three at once. Left and right arrows still work.

Where each dimension has landed

The bar is confidence, not preference. A short bar means the answers so far disagree with each other and the engine will keep asking.

This or that

Two whole pages, both built out of the file as it currently stands, differing on a few values. Pick one. A forced choice between two real things is a better reading than a yes or no on a swatch, because there is no scale to be generous or harsh on. You are only saying which one you would rather have made.

Every dimension the two disagree on takes the result. The one you did not pick loses, and a loss counts for more than a win, same as everywhere else in this engine.

Say what is wrong with the one you picked. Optional, and it counts.

Subtracting what everyone does

This is the step that decides whether the engine is worth anything. Read a pile of work I like and you get a description of good design in general. The part that is mine is what is left after you remove everything true of any competent designer.

What the galleries agree on

Subtracting a consensus means knowing what it is, so I counted one instead of assuming it. These are siteinspire’s own style filters with the number of sites under each, read on 30 October 2026. Minimal is tagged on 847 sites. Pattern is tagged on 19. That ratio is the thing worth noticing: the corpus above is mostly pattern.

Telling it where it is wrong

The kept tags get written as statements specific enough to argue with. I mark each one. The false ones matter most, because a first read of anyone’s taste is a description of good design until they correct it. Marking here rewrites the file in the next step.

Rules in your own words

The claims above were written by the engine out of the corpus. These are written by you, carried into every prompt word for word, and never paraphrased. Say what is good or what is bad, and scope it if it only applies to one kind of thing.

The same taste, four different jobs

    
A shop, a photography site, a long read and a listing, all built from the file
      above. They should not look like each other. A shop is a catalogue with a price on everything;
      a photography site opens on a picture and barely speaks. What they share is the hand, not the
      layout. If one of them looks wrong, that is a judgment or a rule the engine has not been given yet.

    

    

      
      
    

    

  


  
  

    
Putting it to work

    
Say what you are making. The engine wraps it in the file and hands back the
      whole instruction, ready to paste anywhere.

    
The file describes a field, not a single answer, so every run picks different
      values inside it and commits to them. Generate another and the type, the ground and the
      measure change while the refusals hold. That is the difference between a taste and a template.
      Above all of it sit the rules that are not matters of taste at all: contrast, minimum sizes,
      line length, things that have to be true whatever the look.

    
The brief is read first and it outranks the file on anything to do with
      function. The engine also picks only two or three signature moves per run out of everything
      it knows, because using the whole vocabulary at once is the failure, not the goal. Halftone
      belongs in the file. It does not belong on every page.
